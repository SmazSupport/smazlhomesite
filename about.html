About Me

I've spent most of my career in operations, logistics, and fulfillment. Over the years, I've worked across transportation, warehouse management, eCommerce, customer operations, and process improvement, helping teams solve problems and scale efficiently.

As I moved deeper into operations, I became increasingly interested in the systems behind the work. I found that many operational challenges weren't just process problems—they were data, technology, and visibility problems. That curiosity led me to expand into analytics, automation, integrations, and software development.

Today, I sit at the intersection of operations and technology. I enjoy taking complex business processes, understanding how they work in the real world, and using data and technology to make them more efficient, scalable, and easier to manage.

Whether the need is operational leadership, process improvement, data analysis, reporting, system integrations, or light software development, I bring a practical perspective built from hands-on experience and a passion for continuous learning.

Career History

Download Resume
2004 – 2012

Starting on the floor

I got into this the way most operations people do: someone needed help in a warehouse and I was there. At Protege Delivery outside Pittsburgh, I managed warehouse operations for a regional delivery company — inventory accountability, timed delivery routing, driver training, and the customer service calls that come with all of it. Small enough that I did every job in the building at least once, which turned out to be the most useful education I've had.

I moved to North Carolina and spent four years at Courier Express. As Logistics Specialist I coordinated shipments across eight states and oversaw receiving, storage, and shipping operations. That led to Regional Operations Specialist — supervising seven administrative staff and directing 65 contracted drivers across two off-site warehouses. I coordinated receiving, inventory control, storage, distribution, and traffic for a full product range: chemicals, electronics, auto parts, office supplies, and palletized freight. Managing 65 contracted drivers at 26 teaches you that authority is worth very little and credibility is worth everything.

2013 – 2016

Learning the systems

At Vietri, an importer of Italian tableware in Hillsborough, I supervised key account shipping. Fragile, expensive, time-sensitive product — which raises the cost of every mistake. It's also where I first administered an ERP. I ran SAP Business One supporting warehouse shipping, EDI documentation, carrier integrations, and order workflows. I had no background in it. I learned it because the alternative was waiting on someone else to answer questions I needed answered that day.

Once I could query the system myself I could see patterns I'd only suspected — which carriers were quietly underperforming, where the process was lying about itself. That turned out to be the pivot point of my career. I went back to Courier Express in a 3PL role, coordinating FTL, LTL, and Final Mile across carrier networks, building the pricing models behind new business wins, and traveling to vendor sites to audit whether they were actually following the procedures they'd agreed to. Frequently they weren't.

2016 – 2021

Running it at scale

Diligent Delivery Systems is where both halves of my background finally merged. I joined as North Carolina Operations Manager, moved to National Director of Operations across 65 locations, and eventually took on Director of Business Intelligence concurrently — training my successor at each step rather than leaving a hole behind me. The headline number is a 120% increase in freight net profit. The honest explanation is that it wasn't one clever idea — it was workflow redesign, KPI development, carrier performance management, and unglamorous contract renegotiation, all compounding. I secured a $3 million national contract by building the executive reporting and analytics that made our RFP credible in ways competitors' weren't.

The Business Intelligence role ran alongside it: I managed the IT department, directed development of a proprietary delivery management platform, and designed the reporting architecture and KPI frameworks the whole organization ran on. Sitting in both chairs at once was the most valuable thing that's happened to my career. When you own the operation and the reporting on the operation, you lose the ability to hide behind either one.

2021 – 2024

Building it myself

I went independent in 2021, consulting on logistics operations, process improvement, systems integration, and IT infrastructure. During this period I served concurrently as Director of Information Technology at Urban Shipper Technology — directing development of a proprietary logistics management platform from business requirements through architecture, development, testing, and deployment. I designed database structures, API integrations, and technical specifications for dispatch, routing, driver management, and executive reporting, coordinating engineers, QA, and business analysts across the lifecycle.

2024 – Present

Now

I'm Operations Manager at The Woobles, a crochet kit company in Durham with a fulfillment operation that moves between 2,000 and 13,000 orders on a given day. I manage a team of 20 warehouse associates across picking, packing, and shipping, own inventory planning and SKU slotting for 1,500+ active SKUs using demand forecasting, and build the KPI dashboards leadership uses daily.

I've also automated whatever I can — fulfillment workflows built on Shopify APIs, webhooks, Python, and JavaScript, integrating ShipBlink, EasyPost, Airtable, and USPS into processes that used to be manual. I still spend time on the floor. It's the only way to know whether the dashboard is telling the truth.

How I Work

I start by watching.

Process documentation describes what's supposed to happen. Standing at the pack station for an hour tells you what does.

Most operational problems are visibility problems.

Before optimizing anything, I want to know whether anyone can actually see it. Usually they can't — and building the measurement changes behavior before any process change does.

I build the thing rather than requesting it.

Twenty years of waiting on IT queues taught me to learn enough Python, SQL, and API work to solve my own problems. That makes me a better partner to real engineers, not a substitute for them.

I train my replacement.

At every step at Diligent I moved up by making myself unnecessary first. Operations that depend on one person aren't operations — they're a hostage situation.
